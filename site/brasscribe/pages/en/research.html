Research

From a recording to a brass-band score

What Brasscribe does to a recording, how each step was measured, and where the result is still wrong.

Brasscribe project · Every number and figure on this page is read from the repository's benchmark files when the site is built, so it changes when they do · The same for tab: Fretscribe's paper

Abstract

In plain words: Brasscribe hears most of the notes in a brass recording. It is much less sure where they belong in the bar, and its question marks warn about only some of the mistakes. What you get is a draft to correct, not a finished part.

Brasscribe turns a recording into a score and parts for a British-style brass band on the musician's own devices. Learned models only listen: they separate layers, find notes and find beats. Music rules that always give the same result then write: rhythm, note names, key, harmony, parts for the band, difficulty. No listening model was trained for this; the listening stages were chosen by benchmark on recordings with known notes, and the results below are held by a regression gate unless a figure names another source.

On {{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} real brass quartets the best transcriber finds notes with onset F1 {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} at 100 ms ({{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1}} at 50 ms), and {{data brass/urmp-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} on {{data p/urmp-transcription/pieces int}} chamber pieces. Given the score's own beats, the rules put {{span brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of notes in their exact place in the bar; with beats tracked from the audio that falls to {{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc pct}}. End to end on one player's recording, notes are heard with F1 {{span brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.onset_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.onset_f1}}, but only {{span brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.final0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.final0.written_f1}} when a note must also be written where the score has it, and the bar length is right for {{span brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.meter_match brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.final0.meter_match brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.meter_match brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.final0.meter_match pct}} of parts. From a four-part mix the tenor voice is recovered with F1 {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}}, so the inner parts of a band score are arranged, not heard. The doubt marks are rarely false alarms, but they sit on at most {{data brass/solo-instruments/trumpet.q_hit pct}} of a solo line's wrong notes. Beat and bar tracking is the main limit on a readable score.

1 The problem

In shortA player hears a piece and wants the part. Writing it down by ear takes hours. Brasscribe writes a first draft and has to say which notes it is unsure about.

Automatic music transcription turns audio into notes: which pitch sounds, when it starts and when it stops. A musician needs more. A part is readable when its rhythms sit on a beat grid a player can count, in bars of the right length, with the note names a composer would write. It is playable when it stays in the instrument's range, is written in its transposition (B♭ or E♭ for most of the band), and does not cross its neighbours.

A British-style brass band has 18 parts on a contest score, from soprano cornet to B♭ bass and percussion; Norwegian bands use the same layout. Most of the instruments share one family sound and double each other constantly. That is the hard case for every model we know of: none knows the band's instruments, and none tells several players of one instrument apart.

Three constraints shaped the work:

  • The musician's own devices. Recordings are not sent to a cloud service. A full band is processed on the musician's computer. On Android the app writes a solo by itself. On Windows, iPhone, iPad and Mac every score comes from the computer: the app does not yet have what it needs to write one alone.
  • Doubt is shown. A note the system doubts carries a ?question mark, by shape and colour, and the musician checks the marked notes in groups. Section 6.6 measures how much those marks catch.
  • Measured, then gated. A stage is chosen by benchmark on recordings with known correct notes, and a gate fails the build when a number moves.

2 Related work

In shortGood open models exist for each listening task. None was made for brass bands, so each was tested on brass before it was trusted.

Notes from many instruments. MT3 treats multi-instrument transcription as translation from a spectrogram to note tokens[1], and YourMT3+ extended it[2]. MuScriptor is a later open model of the same kind, trained on real multi-instrument recordings[3]. Basic Pitch is a small model that does not care which instrument plays[4]; Onsets and Frames set the pattern it follows[7].

One line. For a single melody a pitch tracker is simpler and more exact than a transcriber. CREPE introduced the learned pitch tracker[6]; SwiftF0 is a very small recent one[5]. Its published ranking comes from a benchmark its own author maintains[24], so we measured it on brass ourselves (section 6.4).

Separating instruments. HT-Demucs[12] and BS-RoFormer[11] split a mix into stems. We use two community checkpoints of the latter: BS-RoFormer SW (six stems) and MVSep Mega-53 (53 stems, among them brass, trumpet, trombone, French horn and tuba)[13][25]. We found no open separator that knows cornet, tenor horn, baritone or euphonium, and none that separates several players of one instrument.

Beats and bars. Earlier beat trackers decode a network's output with a probabilistic model of tempo and bar position[9][10]. Beat This! predicts beats and downbeats directly[8]. No open model gives a time signature: the metre has to be worked out from the spacing of the downbeats.

Note names, key and notation. A pitch number does not say whether a note is F♯ or G♭. The ps13 algorithm names notes from their tonal context[14][15], in partitura's implementation[23]. The key comes from the Krumhansl–Kessler profiles[16]. Scores are written as MusicXML[22].

What was missing. No model knows the band's instruments, no model separates voices of the same sound, and we found no published accuracy on brass-band audio. The evaluation therefore had to be assembled first (section 4), from the nearest real brass recordings that come with their notes.

In shortModels listen and rules write. After the vote between the models, the same notes always give exactly the same score.

Routing: separate first, or not

The musician answers "What is this?", and the answer picks the route; the app does not guess. A solo and a brass band go straight to the note models: in an all-brass recording there is nothing for a separator to split. A pop or rock song is first split into stems with BS-RoFormer SW, and each stem is transcribed. For a soloist with an orchestra, Mega-53 lifts out the solo, the bass and the drums, and the orchestra is what remains of the mix.

The vote, and what a ? means

Every note model hears the same audio, and notes are grouped when they share a pitch and start together. For a solo, SwiftF0 supplies the notes and a second model confirms them; a note SwiftF0 did not hear is dropped (section 6.6 shows why). A small logistic model then turns the evidence for each note into a probability that it is right: which models found it, how long it is, how firmly the pitch tracker held it, and whether the line was separated from a mix. A note below the threshold gets a ?question mark. Neighbouring marked notes share one mark, and the review starts with the musician's own part.

Beats, bars and the grid

Beat This! tracks beats and downbeats on the whole mix. Rules then pick the beat a player would count (the tracker often taps half notes in slow music), tidy the bar lines where the tracker inserted or dropped a beat, and mark passages without a steady pulse as ad lib. The notes are then moved onto the grid: for each beat the quantizer weighs how well a subdivision fits against how complicated it is to read, at 24 ticks per beat. Written note lengths come from where the pitch contour leaves the note, not from how long the player held it.

Names, harmony and parts

Note names are chosen for the whole ensemble at sounding pitch, before anything is transposed, and the key is estimated from the same notes. Dense accompaniment is reduced to the chord tones of each beat. Three arrangers write the parts: a full band, a small band, and a quartet whose two inner parts follow classical part-writing rules (no crossing, no parallel fifths or octaves, only chord tones). Three difficulty levels follow: as played, standard, and easier.

Everything in this section after the vote is written twice, in Python as the reference and in Rust for the apps, and a conformance suite requires the two to produce identical output.

Training and evaluation. Brasscribe trains no listening model: all six are used as published. Two things were fitted on our data. The confidence model was fitted on notes with known answers from these same sets, and checked on a half of the recordings it had not seen. The thresholds in the music rules (the quantizer's penalties, the bar-line cleanup, the duration and staccato rules, the fast-note rules) were chosen while looking at the sets they are scored on. There is no held-out brass set. The numbers below therefore show how well the rules fit these recordings, which is at best an upper bound for a recording made elsewhere.

What the sets are not. None is a brass band. The quartets have one player per part, recorded one at a time; a band has several players per part, in a hall. The largest set has {{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} pieces.

6 Results

In shortMost notes are found. Putting them in the right place in the bar is the weak step. The middle parts of a group are mostly not heard.

6.1 Notes found in a group

{{chart notes-found}}

On a brass quartet the best model reaches F1 {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} at the looser tolerance, and the pieces differ a good deal: from {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@min}} to {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@max}}. The strict 50 ms tolerance costs brass a lot, because a soft attack speaks late; the looser one is the fairer measure of what ends up in the score. On a full band, separating the instruments first scored higher than transcribing the mix, on two synthesized songs.

6.2 Where the notes land

The figure for this is the one under the abstract. It is the central result of the paper. Given the right beats, the rules write {{span brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of the notes exactly where the score has them. Given the beats the tracker found, they manage {{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc pct}}, and the dots show how little a mean says here: single pieces range from {{span brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@max brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@max pct}}, depending on how well the tracker found the beats and the bar lines. Position within the beat holds up much better ({{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.subdivision_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.subdivision_acc pct}}), so the rhythm inside a beat is usually readable even when the bar lines are not. Written lengths are right for {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} of the chamber-music notes even with perfect beats: players shorten notes, and the rules only partly undo that.

One tuning step is behind the left-hand numbers. Making a simple rhythm cheaper for the quantizer to choose than a complicated one moved exact positions on the chorales from {{data m/penalties/position_before pct}} to {{data brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}}, with the score's beats. The step was tuned on these chorales, so that gain is a fit, not a test.{{holds brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc > m/penalties/position_before}}

{{chart durations}}

Three rules for a note's written length were compared. This figure's chamber-music numbers are higher than the {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} in the rhythm figure because the two come from different suites: there a length is taken from the quantizer alone and must equal the score's exactly; here a length rule is applied per part and one tick (1/24 of a beat) is allowed. The one in use reads the end of a note from where its pitch stops, and it is the best of the three by a small margin. The chamber-music result stays low under every rule.

6.4 Instrument by instrument

Alone, every brass instrument is tracked well, the tuba included, and almost never in the wrong octave. The second series shows a fault that is easy to mistake for a model's: without knowing the instrument, the solo path keeps only notes in a cornet's range, so a tuba vanishes and a trombone keeps {{data brass/solo-instruments/trombone.today_recall pct}} of its notes. The pitch tracker was never the limit there.

The figure's second view is the check of SwiftF0 on brass that section 2 promised. The second model matters too: a note is written without a mark only when Basic Pitch confirms it, and on the tuba Basic Pitch agrees with {{data brass/solo-instruments/tuba.bp_agrees pct}} of the line's notes. That leaves {{data brass/solo-instruments/tuba.q_missed f1}} wrong notes without a mark per 100 written notes on the tuba, against {{data brass/solo-instruments/trumpet.q_missed f1}} on the trumpet.

6.5 One voice out of a group

{{chart voices}}

The first view asks whether one player's line can be taken out whole. The other views ask a simpler question, whose notes are missing from the transcription at all: mostly the lower two players'.

A player who records the whole group and wants their own line gets it if they play the top part, and nearly so on the second. The tenor voice, in the middle of the harmony on an instrument that sounds like its neighbours, is found with F1 {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}} by the best model. For this reason Brasscribe writes only the melody and the bass of a band from what it heard, and arranges the parts in between from the harmony.

{{chart inner-parts}}

How good are those arranged parts? Given the true harmony, the quartet arranger chooses the same note as the composer for about four notes in five, with {{data brass/arrange/quartet.parallels_per_100 f2}} parallel fifths or octaves per 100 chord changes against {{data brass/arrange/quartet-reference.parallels_per_100 f2}} in the chorales themselves. Starting from a recording it matches about half, and writes {{data brass/quartet-audio/mus.parallels_per_100 f1}} parallels per 100. The arrangers never write a note an instrument cannot play and never cross parts; that is guaranteed by how they are built, so it shows the rules hold, not that the music is good.

6.6 The doubt marks

{{chart agreement}}

Agreement between models is good evidence. This is why a note needs SwiftF0 and one more model to be written without a mark, and why two models that agree without SwiftF0 are not trusted.

{{chart doubt}}

The marks themselves are a weaker tool than the agreement behind them suggests. A marked note is usually worth looking at: few right notes are marked. But most wrong notes carry no mark, and on the French horn none did.{{holds brass/solo-instruments/horn.q_hit <= 0}} The marks also say nothing about the commonest fault of all, a right note in the wrong place in the bar (6.3): they judge pitch and onset only. A musician should treat an unmarked note as "probably the right pitch", not as "correct".

6.7 Fast notes

{{chart fast-notes}}

Runs, repeated notes and trills are the passages players most want written down, and the hardest. The pitch tracker hears about six of their notes in ten. With the right beats nearly all of those survive to the page; with tracked beats more are lost when the notes are put on the grid. Of the trills and two-note alternations, {{data brass/fast-notes/oracle.written.alt_kept pct}} keep at least four fifths of their notes with the right beats and {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.written.alt_kept pct}} with tracked ones. The rules for fast notes were added after a first version lost most of them; the figure's other views show before and after. They helped, and the passages are still mostly not written.

7 Where it fails

In shortRhythm more than pitch. Inner voices more than outer. Fast and ornamented playing more than plain.

  • Rhythm, not pitch, is the larger error. On a solo, notes are heard with F1 {{data brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.onset_f1}} and written in place with F1 {{data brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.written_f1}} (6.3). Most of the gap is the beat grid: with the score's beats the same rules place {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of correct notes (6.2). It varies enormously from piece to piece: with tracked beats, between {{span brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@max brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@max pct}} of a piece's notes are in place.
  • Bar length. The metre is wrong for most solo parts (6.3).{{holds brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.meter_match < 0.5}}{{holds brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.meter_match < 0.5}} A part in three that is written in four has every bar line in the wrong place, however good its notes.
  • Inner voices. From a quartet the tenor is found with F1 {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}} and the bass with {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.B_f1}}, against {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.S_f1}} for the top voice (6.5).
  • Octave errors are rare where the notes are found at all. Of the played notes in the quartets, {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.octave_err_rate pct}} were missed while MuScriptor wrote a note one or two octaves away at that moment, and {{data brass/chorales-transcription/basic-pitch.octave_err_rate pct}} with Basic Pitch; in chamber brass {{data brass/urmp-transcription/muscriptor-medium.octave_err_rate pct}} and {{data brass/urmp-transcription/basic-pitch.octave_err_rate pct}}. The count includes written notes that belong to another part an octave away, so it overstates the fault. On a single instrument the rate is at most {{data brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err pct}}.{{holds brass/solo-instruments/tuba.octave_err <= brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err}}{{holds brass/solo-instruments/trumpet.octave_err <= brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err}}{{holds brass/solo-instruments/horn.octave_err <= brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err}}{{holds brass/solo-instruments/baritone.octave_err <= brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err}}{{holds brass/solo-instruments/flugelhorn.octave_err <= brass/solo-instruments/trombone.octave_err}}
  • Fast notes lose about four notes in ten before anything is written (6.7).
  • Ornaments become extra notes. The fast-note set has control clips that must stay one note. With tracked beats a fall adds {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-fall.extra}} notes per played note, a scoop {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-scoop.extra}}, a rip {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-rip.extra}}, and a wide downward vibrato {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-vibdown120.extra}}; an ordinary vibrato adds {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-vibrato.extra}}. No control clip was written as a trill.{{holds brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl.trills <= 0}}
  • Triplets have only been checked on computed note times, not on recordings: with 15 ms of timing error, {{data m/triplets/triplet_eighths pct}} of triplet eighths land on their true place. No recorded triplets with a reference are in the sets above.
  • Note lengths. Even with perfect beats, {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} of chamber-music notes get the score's written length, and {{data brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} of chorale notes (6.2).

In shortBigger was not better, and neither was telling the model what it was hearing. These are the comparisons that were run, including the ones that lost.

  • Three models on a separated solo line. 10 Limitations

In shortSmall test sets that are not brass bands, rules tuned on those same sets, and one route that is checked end to end.

  • No brass-band ground truth. Every number comes from quartets, chamber groups, single players or synthesized songs. How a full band in a hall behaves is not measured.
  • No held-out set. The rules' thresholds were chosen on the recordings they are scored on (section 4).
  • Small sets. {{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} chorales, {{data p/urmp-transcription/pieces int}} chamber pieces, {{data m/datasets/slakh_trumpet_songs int}} songs. The chorales are slow and rhythmically simple.
  • Only one route is checked end to end, and without reference notes: the soloist-with-orchestra route must reproduce a frozen output. The solo, brass-band and pop routes are assembled from the same tested parts but have no end-to-end gate.
  • The doubt marks judge pitch and onset only, and miss most wrong notes (6.6).
  • Inner parts are arranged. In a band score only the melody and the bass come from the recording.
  • Fast notes come from samples. The fast-note clips are assembled from single recorded notes, not played by a person, and the rules were developed on them.
  • Speed is known for one laptop (section 9).
  • Agreement counts are small. The precision of one agreement class rests on {{data m/solo_vote/n_bp_mus int}} notes.

11 What is next

  • Beats and bars first. The results say where the score is lost: a better beat grid for a single line, the metre, and a step where the musician confirms the time signature and the first downbeat before the notes are placed.
  • A held-out brass set, so that a tuned rule can be told from a rule that works.
  • Doubt marks that cover rhythm, and that catch more wrong notes at the same number of false alarms.
  • Brass-band data: a band rendered from public-domain scores, and a few real band recordings with corrected scores.
  • Timing on phones.

12 Licences and data

  • Non-commercial. MuScriptor's weights are CC BY-NC 4.0, so full-band scores may only be used non-commercially. Each musician accepts that licence with their own account and downloads the file themselves.
  • Weights without a stated licence. BS-RoFormer SW and Mega-53 are downloaded by the musician's computer from where their makers publish them. Brasscribe re-hosts none of them.
  • Bundled models (SwiftF0, Basic Pitch, Beat This!) are MIT or Apache-2.0.
  • Datasets. ChoraleBricks is CC BY 4.0, and the repository keeps small data derived from it. URMP and Slakh are downloaded on request; the repository holds one small test file derived from 30 seconds of a URMP track. The church response used for the fast-note clips is from OpenAIR, University of York (CC BY 4.0). The licences are in Table 2 and with each reference below.
  • No copyrighted music is published here or shipped. The built-in example is a public-domain hymn tune.
  • Recordings stay with the musician. No recording, and no score written from one, is sent to a cloud service. One thing is sent: ONNX Runtime, Microsoft's library that runs the models in Play for Android and Windows and in parts of the engine, reports its own use to Microsoft with a device identifier.
