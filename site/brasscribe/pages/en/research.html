Research paper

Brasscribe: Transcribing Brass Recordings into Band Scores with Pretrained Models and Deterministic Rules

Kristoffer-Andre Kalliainen · The Brasscribe project · Built {{built}}; every number and figure is read from the repository's benchmark files at that commit · Companion paper: Fretscribe (tablature)

Abstract

We describe and evaluate Brasscribe, a system that transcribes recordings of brass ensembles into notated scores and parts for British-style brass band. Pretrained models for source separation, note transcription, fundamental-frequency estimation and beat tracking are combined, without further training, with deterministic rules for rhythm quantisation, pitch spelling, harmony reduction and arrangement. Each stage is evaluated on ChoraleBricks brass quartets ({{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} pieces), URMP brass chamber music ({{data p/urmp-transcription/pieces int}} pieces), single-instrument recordings and synthesised material, with note-level precision, recall and F-measure as defined in mir_eval and with score-position accuracy for rhythm. The best transcriber reaches a mean onset F-measure of {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1}} at the standard ±50 ms tolerance ({{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} at ±100 ms) on the quartets and {{data brass/urmp-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1}} ({{data brass/urmp-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}}) on the chamber pieces. Given reference beats, the quantiser places {{span brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of notes at their notated position in the bar; with tracked beats it places {{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc pct}}, with large variation between pieces. End to end on single parts, notes are found with F = {{span brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.onset_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.onset_f1}}, whereas the written F-measure, which also requires the correct position in the bar, is {{span brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.final0.written_f1 brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.final0.written_f1}}. The tenor voice of a quartet is recovered with F = {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}}, and the confidence marks flag at most {{data brass/solo-instruments/trumpet.q_hit pct}} of wrong notes. All rule thresholds were tuned on the evaluation sets and no brass-band recording with reference notes was available, so the results are upper bounds. Beat and metre tracking is the main obstacle to readable output.

Keywords: automatic music transcription, brass band, rhythm quantisation, beat tracking, source separation, evaluation.

Plain-language summary: Brasscribe hears most of the notes in a brass recording. It is much less reliable about where they belong in the bar, and its question marks warn about only some of the mistakes. The output is a draft to correct, not a finished part.

1 Introduction

Automatic music transcription (AMT) converts audio into a symbolic representation: which pitches sound, when they start and when they stop {{cite benetos2019amt}}. A musician needs more than a list of notes. A part is readable when its rhythms lie on a beat grid that can be counted, in bars of the correct length, with the pitch spellings a composer would choose; it is playable when it stays within the instrument's range, is written in its transposition, and does not cross neighbouring parts.

The British-style brass band is a demanding target. A contest score has 18 parts, from soprano cornet to B♭ bass and percussion, and Norwegian bands use the same instrumentation. Most of the instruments share one timbre family and double each other constantly. To our knowledge, no openly available transcription model has the band's instruments in its vocabulary, and none separates several players of the same instrument.

Brasscribe addresses the task with pretrained models that only listen (they separate sources, estimate notes and track beats) and with deterministic rules that write the notation. It runs on the musician's own devices; no recording is uploaded to a server. In the builds released at the time of writing, every score, including a solo part, is produced on the musician's computer on all platforms. The Android app transcribes a solo part on the device in builds that bundle the listening models, which the builds released so far did not; on iPhone, iPad and Mac the models are not yet included, and the Windows app has no on-device path.

The central finding is shown in {{ref fig:rhythm}}: the rules place nearly all notes correctly when the beats are known, and about half on average, with wide variation between pieces, when the beats are tracked from the audio.

{{chart rhythm}}

The contributions of this paper are:

  • a description of a complete audio-to-score pipeline for brass ensembles that uses only pretrained models and deterministic rules (Section 3);
  • a stage-by-stage evaluation on real brass recordings with reference notes, reported with the standard note-level metrics and with score-position metrics for rhythm (Sections 4 and 5);
  • negative results and comparisons that determined the design, including models and settings that were rejected (Section 5.8);
  • an error analysis and a statement of threats to validity, notably that all thresholds were tuned on the evaluation data (Sections 6 and 7);
  • a page whose numbers and figures are generated from the regression baselines of the public repository, so that they cannot drift from the measurements (Section 9).

2 Related work

Multi-instrument transcription. MT3 formulates multi-instrument transcription as sequence-to-sequence translation from a spectrogram to note tokens {{cite gardner2022mt3}}, and YourMT3+ extends that approach {{cite chang2024yourmt3}}. MuScriptor is a more recent open model of the same family, trained on real multi-instrument recordings {{cite rouard2026muscriptor}}. Basic Pitch is a lightweight, instrument-agnostic note transcriber {{cite bittner2022basicpitch}} in the onset-and-frame tradition of Onsets and Frames {{cite hawthorne2018onsets}}.

Fundamental-frequency (f0) estimation. For a monophonic line, an f0 estimator is simpler and more precise than a polyphonic transcriber. CREPE introduced a learned f0 estimator operating on the waveform {{cite kim2018crepe}}; SwiftF0 is a small recent model {{cite nieradzik2025swiftf0}}. Its published ranking comes from a benchmark maintained under the same account as the model {{cite pitchbenchmark}}; we therefore measured it on brass ourselves (Section 5.4).

Source separation. Hybrid Transformer Demucs {{cite rouard2023htdemucs}} and the band-split RoPE Transformer (BS-RoFormer) {{cite lu2024bsroformer}} separate a mixture into stems. We use two community checkpoints of the latter architecture: BS-RoFormer SW, with six stems {{cite audioseparator}}, and MVSep Mega-53, with 53 stems that include brass, trumpet, trombone, French horn and tuba {{cite solovyev2026msst}}. We found no open separator with stems for cornet, tenor horn, baritone or euphonium, and none that separates several players of one instrument.

Beat and downbeat tracking. Earlier trackers decode the activations of a neural network with a dynamic Bayesian network over tempo and bar position {{cite krebs2015}}, {{cite bock2016madmom}}. Beat This! predicts beats and downbeats directly {{cite foscarin2024beatthis}}. None of these models outputs a time signature; the metre must be inferred from the spacing of the downbeats.

Pitch spelling, key and notation. A pitch number does not determine whether a note is F♯ or G♭. The ps13 algorithm spells pitches from their tonal context {{cite meredith2006ps13}}; we use the implementation in partitura {{cite cancino2022partitura}}. The key is estimated with the Krumhansl–Kessler profiles {{cite krumhansl1990}}, and scores are written in MusicXML {{cite musicxml}}.

Gap. We found no published accuracy figures for transcription of brass-band audio, and no dataset of brass-band recordings with aligned notes. The evaluation in this paper is therefore assembled from the closest available real brass recordings (Section 4.1).

3 Method

3.1 Overview

{{ref fig:brass-pipeline}} shows the pipeline, and {{ref table:models}} lists the pretrained models and their roles. Learned models are used only for listening; every stage after the vote between the note models is deterministic, so identical notes always yield an identical score.

3.2 Routing

The user states what the recording is, and that answer selects the route. A solo part and a brass ensemble are passed directly to the note models, since an all-brass mixture offers a separator nothing to split. A pop or rock recording is first separated with BS-RoFormer SW, and each stem is transcribed. For a soloist with an orchestra, Mega-53 extracts the solo, the bass and the drums, and the orchestra is taken as the residual of the mixture.

3.3 Note consensus and confidence

The note models process the same audio, and their notes are clustered when they agree in pitch and onset. For a solo part, SwiftF0 supplies the notes and a second model confirms them; a note that SwiftF0 did not produce is discarded (Section 5.6 gives the evidence). A logistic regression then maps the evidence for each note to a probability that it is correct: which models produced it, its duration, the voicing confidence of the f0 contour at its start, and whether the line was separated from a mixture. A note below a threshold is marked with a ?question mark; neighbouring marked notes share one mark.

3.4 Beats, metre and quantisation

Beat This! tracks beats and downbeats on the full mixture. Rules select the metrical level a player would count (the tracker often follows half notes in slow music), repair bar lines where a beat was inserted or dropped, and label passages without a steady pulse as ad lib. The notes are then quantised: for each beat, the quantiser weighs how well a subdivision fits the onsets against how complex it is to read, at a resolution of 24 ticks per beat. Written durations are derived from where the f0 contour leaves the note, not from the performed duration.

3.5 Spelling, harmony and arrangement

Pitches are spelled for the whole ensemble at concert pitch, before transposition, and the key is estimated from the same notes. Dense accompaniment is reduced to the chord tones of each beat. Three arrangers write the parts: a full band, a small band, and a quartet whose inner parts follow part-writing rules (no crossing, no parallel fifths or octaves, chord tones only). Three difficulty levels are derived from the result.

All stages after the vote are implemented twice, in Python as the reference and in Rust for the apps, and a conformance suite requires identical output from the two.

4.2 Splits and data leakage

No listening model was trained or fine-tuned; all six are used as published. Two components were fitted to our data. The confidence model (Section 3.3) was fitted on notes with reference labels from the sets in {{ref table:data}} and validated on a held-out half of the recordings. The thresholds of the rules (the quantiser's complexity penalties, the bar-line repair, the duration and staccato rules, the fast-note rules) were chosen while inspecting the same sets on which they are scored. There is no held-out brass set. The results in Section 5 therefore measure how well the rules fit these recordings and should be read as upper bounds for unseen recordings.

4.3 Metrics

All measures lie between 0 and 1; higher is better. The detection of a note and its placement in the score are measured separately. Note-level measures are computed with mir_eval.transcription {{cite raffel2014mireval}}, onset-only and, where stated, onset+offset. The primary onset tolerance is ±50 ms; ±100 ms is reported as a secondary, relaxed tolerance, and several suites store results at ±100 ms only, which is stated where it applies. An octave error is a reference note that remains unmatched while an estimated note one or two octaves away starts within the onset tolerance; the octave-error rate is the share of reference notes for which this holds.

4.4 Protocol

Model outputs are computed once and stored; scribe bench rescores every suite from the stored outputs and fails when a value has moved by more than the tolerance recorded in eval/baselines.json. Results are means over pieces. Where the per-piece values are available we also report the sample standard deviation (SD) and a 95 % confidence interval (CI) of the mean, obtained with a percentile bootstrap over pieces (10,000 resamples, fixed seed) {{cite efron1993bootstrap}}; with {{data p/urmp-transcription/pieces int}} to {{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} pieces these intervals are wide. No significance tests were carried out, and differences between conditions are reported descriptively. F-measure, precision and recall are given as proportions to two decimals, and shares as percentages (with one decimal below 10 %); the parity of converted models is given to three decimals because its threshold is 0.98. Precision and recall are reported beside the F-measure where the baselines store them. Most figures read the file used by the gate; figures built from other files (the model-conversion reports, and a small set of values measured once, listed in the Appendix) state this in their source line.

5 Results

5.1 Note detection in ensembles

{{ref fig:notes-found}} gives the onset F-measure of the two transcribers. On the quartets the best model reaches {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1}} at the standard ±50 ms tolerance (SD {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1@sd}}, 95 % CI {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1@lo}}–{{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1@hi}}) and {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} at the relaxed ±100 ms tolerance (SD {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@sd}}, 95 % CI {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@lo}}–{{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@hi}}; range {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@min}}–{{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1@max}}). The baselines of these suites store the F-measure only, so precision and recall are not reported separately here. The difference between the two tolerances is large for brass, consistent with late detection of soft attacks. On the two synthesised songs with a trumpet lead, separating before transcription scored higher than transcribing the mixture; with two songs this is suggestive only.

5.2 Rhythm quantisation

{{ref fig:rhythm}} (Section 1) isolates the rhythm stage by quantising the reference notes. With reference beats, {{span brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of notes are written at their notated position in the bar. With tracked beats the mean falls to {{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc pct}}, and single pieces range from {{span brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.position_acc@max brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@min brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.position_acc@max pct}}; the mean is therefore a poor summary. Position within the beat is more robust ({{span brass/quant-urmp/beat_this.subdivision_acc brass/quant-chorales/beat_this.subdivision_acc pct}}), so the rhythm inside a beat is usually readable even when the bar lines are displaced. One tuning step underlies the reference-beat results: scaling the quantiser's complexity penalties raised exact positions on the chorales from {{data m/penalties/position_before pct}} to {{data brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc pct}}. The step was tuned on these chorales, so the gain is a fit and not a test.{{holds brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.position_acc > m/penalties/position_before}}

The tracked beats were also scored directly with the beat F-measure of mir_eval.beat (tolerance ±70 ms). No annotated beats exist for these sets, so the reference is the series of quarter-note beats interpolated from the notated positions of the reference notes. The raw output of Beat This! reaches a mean F-measure of {{data p/quant-chorales/beat_this.beat_f@mean}} on the quartets (SD {{data p/quant-chorales/beat_this.beat_f@sd}}, 95 % CI {{data p/quant-chorales/beat_this.beat_f@lo}}–{{data p/quant-chorales/beat_this.beat_f@hi}}) and {{data p/quant-urmp/beat_this.beat_f@mean}} on the chamber pieces (SD {{data p/quant-urmp/beat_this.beat_f@sd}}, 95 % CI {{data p/quant-urmp/beat_this.beat_f@lo}}–{{data p/quant-urmp/beat_this.beat_f@hi}}). A tracker that follows another metrical level than the quarter note is penalised by this reference. Downbeat F-measure and the continuity measures (CMLt, AMLt) were not computed.

Written durations are examined separately in {{ref fig:durations}}, which compares three rules for deriving a written duration. The rule in use is the best of the three by a small margin, and the chamber-music result remains low under every rule. These values are higher than the duration accuracy in {{ref fig:rhythm}} ({{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} for chamber music) because the suites differ: there the duration is taken from the quantiser alone and must equal the notated one exactly, whereas here a duration rule is applied per part and a deviation of one tick is accepted.

5.5 Individual voices of an ensemble

{{ref fig:voices}} asks whether the line of one player can be extracted from a quartet recording by the range of the instrument. The top voice is recovered and the tenor largely is not (F = {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}} with the best model). The further views give the recall of each voice's notes anywhere in the transcription; these are upper bounds, and the lower two voices are the ones most often missing. For this reason the system writes only the melody and the bass of an ensemble from the audio and arranges the parts between them from the harmony.

{{chart voices}}

{{ref fig:inner-parts}} evaluates those arranged parts against the composer's. Given the harmony of the score, the quartet arranger chooses the composer's pitch class for about four notes in five, with {{data brass/arrange/quartet.parallels_per_100 f2}} parallel fifths or octaves per 100 chord changes, against {{data brass/arrange/quartet-reference.parallels_per_100 f2}} in the chorales themselves. From a recording it matches about half, with {{data brass/quartet-audio/mus.parallels_per_100 f1}} parallels per 100. That the arrangers never write an unplayable note or cross parts holds by construction and is not evidence of musical quality.

5.6 Agreement between models and the confidence marks

{{ref fig:agreement}} shows that agreement between models is strong evidence that a note is correct, provided that SwiftF0 is one of the models. This motivates the rule of Section 3.3. The marks derived from it are weaker ({{ref fig:doubt}}): few correct notes are marked, but most wrong notes carry no mark, and on the French horn none did.{{holds brass/solo-instruments/horn.q_hit <= 0}} The recordings in {{ref fig:doubt}} are those on which the confidence model was fitted, so performance on new recordings should be expected to be no better. The marks judge pitch and onset only; they say nothing about the most frequent error, a correct note at the wrong position in the bar (Section 5.3).

5.7 Fast passages

{{ref fig:fast-notes}} follows the notes of fast figures (runs, repeated notes, trills) through the stages of the solo path. The f0-based note segmentation retains about six notes in ten. With reference beats nearly all of these reach the score; with tracked beats more are lost at quantisation. Of the trills and two-note alternations, {{data brass/fast-notes/oracle.written.alt_kept pct}} keep at least four fifths of their notes with reference beats and {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.written.alt_kept pct}} with tracked beats. The further views show the effect of the rules that were added for fast notes: recall of the figure's notes improved and remains below one half. The clips are assembled from samples and the rules were developed on them.

5.8 Model and design comparisons

The comparisons that determined the design are summarised in {{ref fig:models}}, {{ref fig:solo-trackers}}, {{ref fig:consensus}} and {{ref fig:melody}}.

{{chart models}}
  • Model size. MuScriptor large scored lower than medium on the quartets ({{ref fig:models}}); medium is used.
  • Instrument conditioning. Conditioning MuScriptor on brass instruments lowered its onset F-measure from {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset_f1}} to {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium-brass.onset_f1}}; conditioning is not used.
  • Retuning. Shifting the recording to A = 440 Hz before Basic Pitch raised its F-measure from {{data brass/chorales-transcription/basic-pitch.onset100_f1}} to {{data brass/chorales-transcription/basic-pitch-retuned.onset100_f1}} at ±100 ms; the combined result of both models did not change ({{data brass/consensus-chorales/best.onset100_f1}} and {{data brass/consensus-chorales-retuned/best.onset100_f1 f2}}).
  • Separation before transcription scored {{data brass/slakh-transcription/pipeB-sw-muscriptor.onset100_f1}} against {{data brass/slakh-transcription/muscriptor-medium.onset100_f1}} on the mixture, on {{data m/datasets/slakh_trumpet_songs int}} synthesised songs ({{ref fig:notes-found}}, third view).
  • Beat-tracker size made little difference on single parts (Section 5.3); phones keep the smaller checkpoint.

6 Discussion

6.1 Error analysis

  • Rhythm is the dominant error. On single parts the onset F-measure is {{data brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.onset_f1}} and the written F-measure {{data brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.written_f1}} (Section 5.3). Most of the difference is attributable to the beat grid: with reference beats the same rules place {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.position_acc pct}} of correct notes (Section 5.2).
  • Metre. The bar length is wrong for most single parts.{{holds brass/solo-beat-model/urmp.small0.meter_match < 0.5}}{{holds brass/solo-beat-model/choralebricks.small0.meter_match < 0.5}} A part in triple time written in quadruple time has every bar line misplaced, whatever the quality of its notes.
  • Inner voices. From a quartet, the tenor is recovered with F = {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.T_f1}} and the bass with {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.B_f1}}, against {{data brass/seat-voices/muscriptor-medium.S_f1}} for the top voice (Section 5.5).
  • Octave errors are infrequent. Of the reference notes of the quartets, {{data brass/chorales-transcription/muscriptor-medium.octave_err_rate pct}} were missed while MuScriptor produced a note one or two octaves away at the same time, and {{data brass/chorales-transcription/basic-pitch.octave_err_rate pct}} with Basic Pitch; for chamber music the rates are {{data brass/urmp-transcription/muscriptor-medium.octave_err_rate pct}} and {{data brass/urmp-transcription/basic-pitch.octave_err_rate pct}}. The count includes estimated notes that belong to another part an octave away and therefore overstates the error.
  • Fast passages lose about four notes in ten before notation (Section 5.7).
  • Ornaments are split into extra notes. On control clips that should remain one note, with tracked beats, a fall adds {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-fall.extra}} notes per reference note, a scoop {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-scoop.extra}}, a rip {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-rip.extra}} and a wide downward vibrato {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-vibdown120.extra}}; an ordinary vibrato adds {{data brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl-vibrato.extra}}. No control clip was notated as a trill.{{holds brass/fast-notes/small0.ctl.trills <= 0}}
  • Triplets were examined only on computed onset times, not on recordings: with 15 ms of timing jitter, {{data m/triplets/triplet_eighths pct}} of triplet eighth notes are placed correctly. The evaluation sets contain no recorded triplets with a reference.
  • Durations. With reference beats, {{data brass/quant-urmp/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} of chamber-music notes and {{data brass/quant-chorales/reference_beats.duration_acc pct}} of chorale notes receive the notated duration (Section 5.2).

6.2 Threats to validity

  • Tuning on the test data. The thresholds of the rules were selected on the recordings on which they are scored, and the confidence marks are evaluated on their fitting data (Section 4.2). The reported values are optimistic to an unknown degree.
  • Sample size. The sets have {{data p/urmp-transcription/pieces int}} to {{data p/chorales-transcription/pieces int}} pieces, and the route comparison on a full band rests on {{data m/datasets/slakh_trumpet_songs int}} synthesised songs. The confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, and one agreement class contains {{data m/solo_vote/n_bp_mus int}} notes.
  • Representativeness. The chorales are slow and rhythmically simple and were recorded one player at a time; neither they nor the chamber pieces resemble a full band in a hall.
  • Synthetic material. The fast-note clips are assembled from single sampled notes, and the full-band songs are synthesised.
  • Tolerance. Headline values use a ±100 ms onset tolerance, which is more lenient than the ±50 ms convention; both are reported where the suites provide them.
  • Measurement. Timing results come from single runs on one computer; some values were measured once and are not gated (Appendix).

7 Limitations

  • No brass-band recording with reference notes was evaluated; behaviour on a full band is not measured.
  • Only one route is checked end to end, and without reference notes: the soloist-with-orchestra route must reproduce a frozen output. The solo, ensemble and pop routes are composed of the same tested components but have no end-to-end gate.
  • In an ensemble score only the melody and the bass are transcribed; the inner parts are arranged.
  • The confidence marks cover pitch and onset only and miss most wrong notes.
  • Computational cost is known for one laptop and for no phone.

8 Conclusion and future work

Pretrained models detect most notes in brass recordings, and deterministic rules notate them correctly when the beat grid is correct. The evaluation indicates that the score is lost mainly at beat and metre tracking, in particular for single parts, and that inner voices of an ensemble are not recoverable with the models examined. The confidence marks are precise but have low recall.

Future work follows from these results: beat and metre estimation for monophonic lines, including a step in which the user confirms the time signature and the first downbeat; a held-out brass set, so that a tuned rule can be distinguished from one that generalises; confidence estimates that cover rhythm; brass-band data, both rendered from public-domain scores and recorded with corrected scores; and measurements of computational cost on phones.

9 Reproducibility statement

  • Code. The system, the benchmarks and this page are in the public repository github.com/181192/brasscribe. This page was built from the commit named under the title.
  • Commands. pixi run scribe bench list lists the suites; pixi run scribe bench cpu rescores all of them from stored model outputs and compares them with eval/baselines.json; pixi run python site/shared/research/per_piece.py produces the per-piece values; site/build.sh builds this page and fails if a stated finding no longer holds.
  • Reproducible from the repository alone. The following results are rescored in continuous integration on every change, from pinned ChoraleBricks annotations and cached model outputs kept in the repository, and can be reproduced without downloading a dataset: the quartet views of {{ref fig:notes-found}} and {{ref fig:rhythm}}, {{ref fig:models}}, {{ref fig:solo-instruments}}, {{ref fig:doubt}}, the first view of {{ref fig:voices}}, {{ref fig:inner-parts}}, the stage view of {{ref fig:fast-notes}}, and the chorale series of {{ref fig:durations}}. {{ref fig:speed}} and {{ref fig:conversions}} are read from committed reports; rerunning them requires the models.
  • Requiring a local download. Results on URMP (the chamber views, {{ref fig:solo-written}}), on Slakh and on separated stems ({{ref fig:agreement}}, {{ref fig:solo-trackers}}, {{ref fig:consensus}}, {{ref fig:melody}}) need the datasets and the models on a local computer; the per-piece values and the beat F-measure need the evaluation data as well. The frozen output of the commercial recording cannot be reproduced by a reader.
  • Data availability. ChoraleBricks, URMP and Slakh are available from their publishers ({{ref table:data}}). The commercial recording is not distributed.
  • Models. All models are public; MuScriptor's weights require acceptance of its licence.
  • Not gated. The values listed in the Appendix were measured once; their sources and commands are recorded in site/shared/research/measured/measured.toml.
