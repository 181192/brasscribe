How it works

From a recording to a brass-band score

The technique behind Brasscribe: which models listen, which rules write the notes down, and how every choice was measured.

Brasscribe project · Written 27 September 2026 · Numbers as of commit 14730d0 · Sources in the repository

Abstract

In plain words: you give Brasscribe a recording, and you get back a brass-band score with the doubtful notes marked for you to check.

Brasscribe turns a recording into a score and parts for a British-style brass band, and it runs on the musician's own devices. No open model knows the brass-band instruments, and none separates several players of the same instrument. So we split the problem. Learned models only listen: they separate layers, find notes and find beats. Deterministic music rules then do the writing: rhythm, note names, key, harmony, voicing for the band and difficulty. On a solo, three transcribers (MuScriptor, Basic Pitch, SwiftF0) each listen, and a logistic model turns their agreement into a calibrated probability that the note is right. That probability decides which notes the musician is asked to check.

On real brass-quartet recordings (ChoraleBricks) the best single transcriber finds notes with onset F1 0.75 at 100 ms, and 0.88 on URMP brass chamber music. Notes the models agree on are 89–99 % correct. With a correct beat grid the quantizer puts 95–99 % of notes in their exact notated position. The symbolic stages are written once in Python and ported to Rust. On 27 September 2026 the port reproduced all 378 conformance cases byte for byte. The listening models run on Apple, Android and Windows devices after conversion, each gated at note F1 ≥ 0.98 against its reference framework. We report where this fails: same-timbre inner voices, unisons, the beat grid on held chords, and pedal notes below 46.9 Hz.

1 Introduction

In shortA brass-band player hears a piece and wants the part. Writing it down by ear takes hours. Brasscribe does the first draft and says honestly which notes it is unsure about.

Automatic music transcription (AMT) is the task of turning audio into symbolic notes: which pitch sounds, when it starts and when it stops. A musician needs more than that. A score is readable when the rhythms sit on a beat grid a player can count, the notes carry the names a composer would write (F♯ or G♭), and the key signature matches the harmony. A part is playable when it stays in the instrument's range, is written in its transposition (B♭ or E♭ for most brass-band instruments), and does not cross or collide with its neighbours.

A British-style brass band has 18 parts on a contest score: soprano cornet, solo, repiano, 2nd and 3rd cornets, flugelhorn, three tenor horns, two baritones, two tenor trombones and a bass trombone, euphonium, E♭ and B♭ basses, and percussion. Norwegian brass bands use the same layout. Most of these instruments share one family timbre, and the parts double each other all the time.

We set three constraints:

  • Local first. A solo recording becomes a part on the phone, offline. A full band is processed on the musician's own computer. Nothing is sent to a cloud service.
  • Honest uncertainty. Every note carries a probability of being right. Doubtful notes are marked with a ?question mark (shape and colour), and the musician checks them in groups.
  • Measured, not assumed. Every stage was chosen by benchmark on recordings with known correct notes, and a regression gate protects each result.

This article describes the system as of 27 September 2026. The work behind it ran from 25 to 27 September 2026: desk research, model adapters and benchmarks on the first day, the Rust port and the apps on the second, and calibrated confidence, the quartet and the band sounds on the third.

2 Background and related work

In shortGood open models exist for each listening task. None of them was made for brass bands, so each had to be tested on brass before we trusted it.

Multi-instrument transcription

MT3 cast multi-instrument transcription as sequence-to-sequence: a Transformer reads a spectrogram and writes MIDI-like tokens with instrument labels[1]. YourMT3+ extended the architecture with a mixture-of-experts encoder and cross-dataset stem augmentation, and was the strongest published model on Slakh and URMP in 2024[2]. MuScriptor (ISMIR 2026) is a decoder-only Transformer with open weights, trained on about 11,000 hours of real multi-instrument recordings and then post-trained with reinforcement learning[3]. On its authors' real-music test set it roughly doubles YourMT3+ in multi-instrument F1 (48.2 against 21.9). Its token format cannot hold two notes of the same pitch on the same instrument at the same time. That limit matters for brass bands, where unisons are everywhere.

Basic Pitch is a small instrument-agnostic model that predicts onsets, frame activations and a pitch contour[4]. Onsets and Frames established the onset-plus-frame design for piano[7].

Monophonic pitch

For a single line, a pitch tracker is simpler and more precise than a polyphonic transcriber. CREPE introduced a convolutional pitch estimator trained on raw audio[6]. SwiftF0 is a very small model (about 135 kB upstream; our static-shape ONNX conversion is 1.1 MB) that led a recent pitch-tracker benchmark with pitch F1 0.778 averaged over ten corpora and eight recording conditions[5][27]. That benchmark is maintained by the SwiftF0 author, and SwiftF0 was trained on its training split, so we treated the ranking as a hint and measured SwiftF0 on brass ourselves.

Source separation

HT-Demucs combines a waveform and a spectrogram U-Net with a cross-domain Transformer[12]. BS-RoFormer splits the spectrogram into frequency bands and runs Transformers with rotary position embeddings across time and bands[11]. We use two community checkpoints of that architecture: BS-RoFormer SW (six stems: vocals, bass, drums, guitar, piano, other) and MVSep Mega-53, which outputs 53 stems including brass, trumpet, trombone, French horn and tuba[13][29]. We found no open separation model that knows cornet, tenor horn, baritone or euphonium, and none that separates several players of one instrument.

Beat, downbeat and meter

Classical beat trackers decode network activations with a dynamic Bayesian network over tempo and bar position[9][10]. Beat This! drops that post-processing and predicts beats and downbeats directly with a Transformer[8]. No open model outputs a time signature, so meter has to come from downbeat spacing and a human check.

Pitch spelling, key and notation

MIDI numbers do not say whether a note is F♯ or G♭. Meredith's ps13 algorithm spells pitches from their tonal context[15][16], and we use the implementation in partitura[23]. Global key comes from the Krumhansl–Kessler key profiles[17]. Scores are written as MusicXML 4.0[22] with music21[24].

Arrangement

Writing a band part from a recording is partly arrangement: the recording may be an orchestra with a soloist, and a quartet may have to play a full-band texture. We found no open arranger with hard range and transposition constraints for brass-band instruments. Brasscribe therefore arranges with deterministic, rule-based voicing, described in section 3.

What was missing

Three gaps shaped the design. No model knows brass-band instruments; every vocabulary stops at General MIDI brass. No model separates same-timbre voices, so three cornets in close harmony come out as one stream. And we found no published accuracy figures on real brass-band audio, so the evaluation set had to be assembled first. Audio language models were ruled out as note sources: published tests show they cannot reliably hear pitch.

3 System overview

In shortModels listen and rules write. After the vote, the same notes always give exactly the same score, every time, on every device.

Capture and routing

Input is an audio or video file, the microphone, or sound playing on the computer. On macOS a Core Audio process tap records one app by its bundle ID; a loopback test captured a 10 s, 440 Hz sine as 10.000 s at 440.0 Hz. DRM-protected playback is blocked on every platform, and the app says so instead of recording silence.

The musician then answers "What is this?": solo, brass band, soloist with orchestra, or pop/rock. The answer picks a profile; the app never guesses silently. The key design choice is whether to separate first:

  • Brass only (pipeline A). The whole mix goes straight to the transcribers. On an all-brass recording there is nothing for a separator to split, and it only adds artefacts.
  • Full band (pipeline B). BS-RoFormer SW splits pop and rock into six stems, and each stem is transcribed. For a soloist with an orchestra, Mega-53 pulls out the solo, bass and drums, and the orchestra is taken as the residual: the mix minus those stems. We did this because Mega-53's string stems were near-silent on our orchestral test recording even though the strings were prominent.

Per-stem transcription

Each layer is transcribed by the models that measured best for it (section 6). On mixes and first-stage stems MuScriptor medium leads, without instrument conditioning. On a separated solo stem SwiftF0 is the spine: its pitch contour is cut into notes by dynamic programming, and MuScriptor or Basic Pitch confirm them. Instrument labels from the models are treated as hints only. MuScriptor labelled our orchestral test's trumpet soloist "distorted electric guitar"; PANNs[14] tagged trumpet at 0.6–0.7 in most 15 s windows.

Consensus and calibrated confidence

Notes from all sources are clustered when they share a pitch and their onsets fall within 100 ms. In the first version a note's confidence was

c = 1 − ∏m (1 − pm)

over the supporting models m, where pm is that model's precision, estimated leaving one song out. The stems of one model vote once, because separation bleed makes them dependent. Rejected candidates are kept as alternatives.

Since 27 September 2026 a solo note's confidence is a calibrated probability that it is right. A logistic regression, fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares with L2 regularisation of 0.01, takes these features:

  • the agreement class: MuScriptor, Basic Pitch and their interaction, with SwiftF0 as the spine;
  • log note duration;
  • how well the SwiftF0 contour supports the note in its first 0.3 s;
  • whether the solo is a separated stem.

It was fitted on notes with ground truth: 1,175 notes from separated solo stems and 21,551 notes from single URMP and ChoraleBricks parts. The data was split into halves by recording, and the model was checked on the held-out half. A ?question mark appears when the probability of being wrong is at least 0.241, that is, below confidence 0.759. The threshold was chosen so that about 9 % of notes on clean recordings are marked; those marks catch about two thirds of the errors. Below confidence 0.4 the mark is boxed. We have not computed a calibration error (ECE) or reliability diagram, so we make no claim about calibration quality beyond this.

Grouped review. Neighbouring marked notes in one voice form one review item: at most one unmarked note between them, no rest of a beat or more, and no more than two bars in all. The group gets one ?question mark at its first note and a dashed bracket. On the orchestral test arrangement, recalibrating the confidence and grouping the marks together turned 215 marked Solo Cornet notes into 83 marks to check. The review starts with the musician's own part.

Beat, meter and free time

Beat This! runs on the whole mix. Three rule-based steps follow.

  • Metrical-level selection. Beat This! often ticks the half-note level on slow music. choose_level doubles the grid when the tempo is under 90 BPM and the median gap between onsets is at most 0.3 beats.
  • Bar-line cleanup. Inserted or dropped beats make the bar count drift. The cleanup is applied only when the tracker's own downbeat labels agree better afterwards, by at least 0.02, or on tracks with one beat per bar and at least three edits.
  • Free time. A passage is free when at least 3 beat intervals and at least 4 s in a row match neither the piece's beat nor its double, half or split, and their spread is at least 0.2 of the mean interval. It is written ad lib. with dashed bar lines, at a local tempo that makes the median melody gap a half note (clamped to 40–100 BPM), with only quarters and eighths inside.

Quantization

Onsets are warped onto the beat grid, piecewise linear between beats. For each beat the quantizer picks a subdivision g ∈ {1, 2, 4, 3, 6} that minimises the squared snap error plus a complexity penalty (0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.06 and 0.12 respectively), at a resolution of 24 ticks per beat. Scaling the penalties up by 20 lifted exact positions on the chorales from 0.89 to 0.99 with true beats: played music is rarely exactly on the beat, and a reader prefers the simpler rhythm.

Durations from the contour

Players shorten notes. Performed length is a poor guide to written length (0.54 agreement on URMP), so written length is inferred. A note ends where the SwiftF0 contour leaves its pitch or its level drops. A note is held to the next onset if it filled at least half the gap and the gap is at most 18 ticks. Otherwise it gets the nearest readable value, and it is marked staccato when it sounded for less than half its written length.

Spelling and key

ps13 (partitura's ps13s1) spells the whole ensemble at once, at concert pitch, before any transposition. Double flats that ps13 emits in dense chromatic runs are rewritten with single accidentals. The global key is the Krumhansl–Kessler estimate on the same notes. Key changes come from a Viterbi pass over bars, where each bar's cost is the share of its notes outside a key signature, and each change costs half a bar. The mode is named from the Krumhansl–Kessler tonic's degree in the signature's scale, so a lydian passage is not forced into major.

Harmony reduction

Dense accompaniment is reduced to one pitch-class set per beat. Each pitch class is weighted by overlap and confidence. The top four are kept (three for the brass choir), as long as each weighs at least 0.35 of the strongest. Identical neighbouring sets merge into one harmony slot.

Arrangement

Arrangement reads only the canonical Composition: concert pitch, ticks, and voices tagged with a role, a confidence and their sources. There are three arrangers.

  • Small band (8 parts). The melody goes to Solo Cornet, one octave choice per phrase. The bass goes to E♭ Bass in its lowest comfortable octave, with B♭ Bass an octave below where that stays readable. Inner parts are voiced greedily per slot: under the melody, over the bass, no crossing, least movement.
  • Layered solo with band (18 parts), for a soloist with an orchestra. The orchestra residual is split by behaviour, not timbre. Short notes attacked together with at least two others become chordal hits for the brass choir (repiano, 2nd and 3rd cornets, trombones). Everything else becomes lines and pads for flugelhorn, horns and baritones. The top moving string line becomes a euphonium countermelody.
  • Quartet: 1st and 2nd cornet, tenor horn and euphonium. The quartet voicer tries every alto–tenor pair in the two parts' ranges. Its hard rules are S > A ≥ T > B, at most an octave between neighbouring upper voices, and chord tones only. Among the legal pairs it minimises, in lexicographic order: chord tones left out (a missing fifth is allowed), parallel fifths and octaves over all six voice pairs, doubling, and movement. Putting parallels before doubling mattered: in the other order the voicer moved in octaves with the bass.

Difficulty and output

Three difficulty modes exist. As played keeps every note. Standard merges pairs of sixteenths into eighths where the dropped note is not a chord tone, and folds every part into its reading range. Easier puts the accompaniment on an eighth-note grid, trims four semitones off the top of each range, and penalises key changes more.

Output is MusicXML with transposing parts and instrument-sound IDs, plus PDF, MIDI and MP3 through the MuseScore command line (optional, never bundled), braille music as BRF through music21's translator, and a talking score in English and Norwegian.

4 Models

In shortEight models, each run as it was published. The small ones run on phones; the large ones need the musician's computer.

Each model runs as its upstream package in an isolated environment, behind a file contract: audio in; MIDI, JSON and a sidecar out. For devices, only the network is swapped for a converted one; resampling, chunking and note creation stay the upstream code. Each conversion is compared with the reference framework's own output: note F1 for transcribers, beat and downbeat F1 at 50 ms for Beat This!, and for separators the F1 of SwiftF0 and Basic Pitch on the converted stems. A model is switched on in the apps only after it passes F1 ≥ 0.98.

Why Slakh is not notation ground truth. Slakh is rendered from live-played Lakh MIDI. Only 12–25 % of one track's trumpet onsets sit on any grid from a quarter to a 1/12 beat, so it cannot score a quantizer. Both transcribers also put every Slakh bass note 12 semitones below the MIDI. pYIN on the rendered stems (one-off) confirmed that the bass is rendered an octave below the written MIDI (median MIDI 39 in the audio against 51 in the file), so our loader shifts bass stems down an octave.

References we corrected. URMP's file tags are sometimes wrong: one trombone is filed as a trumpet. So tracks are matched by number. ChoraleBricks' pitch_name field is naive MIDI naming; spelling is scored against pitch_written_name. URMP score MIDI stores a quarter note as 23/24 of a beat, which produced 64th rests until tiny gaps were held.

Metrics. Note metrics use mir_eval[21]: onset F1 at 50 ms (the standard), and also at 100 ms, because soft brass attacks speak late. On a separated trumpet stem the median onset was 55 ms after the MIDI note-on (one-off). Anything under a sixteenth note (about 125 ms at that tempo) is absorbed by quantization anyway. F1 combines two questions into one number between 0 and 1: of the notes we wrote, how many were right (precision), and of the notes that were played, how many we found (recall). An onset counts as found when it lands within the tolerance at the right pitch. We also report onset-plus-offset F1, octave-error rate, per-part recall, exact notated position, position within the beat, and written-duration accuracy (±1 tick). For arrangement we report melody kept, bass kept, harmony fidelity, voice crossings, notes outside a playable range, alto and tenor recall against the chorales' own inner voices, and parallels per 100 voice changes (parallel fifths or octaves, which part-writing avoids).

Gates. brasscribe bench reruns every suite from cached model outputs and fails if any metric moves more than 0.01 from eval/baselines.json. Counts such as crossings are gated exactly. The ChoraleBricks suites run in CI against sha256-pinned annotations. URMP, Slakh and the orchestral test recording run locally only, because their redistribution terms are unchecked or the audio is commercial.

Conformance. The same gate applies across languages. The Rust port must reproduce the Python reference byte for byte: composition.json with bit-equal floats, canonicalised MusicXML, talking scores in both languages, and humanised playback.

For a musician: the best model finds about three notes in four in a brass quartet and nearly nine in ten in chamber music. It misses more in the inner and low voices.

MuScriptor medium was the best single model. The large model was erratic rather than better: it beat medium on one chorale (0.617 against 0.604) and collapsed on another (0.388 against 0.532). Running it in float32 instead of float16 changed nothing. Telling MuScriptor that the instruments are brass cost 23 F1 points, so it is never conditioned. The models complement each other. At 50 ms, MuScriptor recalled the soprano at 0.84 but the tenor at only 0.48 and the bass at 0.45, while Basic Pitch caught the tenor at 0.71 with low precision.

Separation. On the Slakh trumpet-lead songs, separating first (pipeline B) beat transcribing the mix. SW separation followed by MuScriptor reached onset F1 0.70 at 100 ms, against 0.56 for MuScriptor on the mix and 0.47 for Basic Pitch on the mix (25 September 2026, after the bass-octave fix). Before the Slakh bass-octave fix, pipeline B led by 8–12 F1 points. On brass-only chorales separation is unnecessary.

Solo stems. On solo stems separated by Mega-53 (10 chorales and 2 Slakh songs), SwiftF0 alone scored onset F1 0.75, MuScriptor 0.50 and Basic Pitch 0.42. MuScriptor hallucinated on second-stage stems: 0.00 on three chorales, and 2,672 "piano" notes on one trumpet stem (one-off). On a clean isolated URMP trumpet, SwiftF0 scored 0.994.

For a musician: a note that two models heard is almost always right. A note only one model heard is right about half the time or less, so it gets a question mark.

On mixes, notes that MuScriptor and Basic Pitch agree on were 92 % precise on the chorales and 89 % on URMP, where the consensus at threshold 0.8 tied MuScriptor's F1 (0.88) with higher precision. On Slakh, consensus did not beat pipeline B on F1 (0.63 against 0.70), but at threshold 0.7 it recalled 95 % of the trumpet and 99.5 % of the bass. This is why agreement became the confidence signal and not a filter. The adopted solo rule is that SwiftF0 supplies the notes, a second model confirms them, and notes without SwiftF0 are dropped. On the orchestral test recording, that rule cut the share of unconfirmed solo notes from 78 % to 31 %.

Overlaps and in-between pitches

A one-off study on 27 September 2026 (docs/research/11-overlaps-and-in-between-tones.md) classed every reference note by its overlap with other parts.

  • Unisons are the biggest overlap loss. Recall of unison notes was 0.48–0.61, against 0.82–0.92 for notes with no overlap. The models do hear the unison pitch (88–93 % of the time), but only once. What is missing is "how many players", which is a symbolic problem.
  • Octaves cost little with MuScriptor. The chorale tuba's recall is the same with and without an octave above it (0.72 against 0.71). Its deficit comes from the low register and intonation, not masking.
  • Tuning matters for Basic Pitch. The 18 eval pieces sit between −1 and +20 cents from A440, nearly all sharp, and so does the orchestral test's soloist (+20 cents, about A = 445 Hz). On chorale tuba notes more than 50 cents off the written pitch, Basic Pitch recall is 0.03 and MuScriptor's 0.52.
  • A metric flaw. The octave-error rate counted a correctly found partner voice as an octave error. Counting only unmatched estimates, the chorale rates are 2.4 % for MuScriptor and 7.0 % for Basic Pitch (against 2.7 % and 10.8 % in Table 3's source), and on URMP 0.6 % and 2.4 %.

For a musician: rhythm inside the beat is mostly right. Bar lines can drift, so check where each bar starts.

The quantizer itself is sound: with true beats, 95–99 % of notes land on their exact notated position. The weak link is the beat grid. On chorales, Beat This! inserts extra beats during fermatas (1.1–1.2 detected beats per notated quarter), so bar positions drift even though positions within the beat stay right. It picked the half-note level on 5 of 8 URMP pieces. Metrical-level selection fixed 4 of the 6 half-level tracks across both sets with no false doubles. The bar-line cleanup halved drift events (268 to 139). Its thresholds were tuned on the same data.

Free time (26 September 2026). The detector fires on 1 of 18 eval pieces: a ritardando into a 4.5 s hold at the end of Air on the G String. Scores on the strict passages are unchanged. On the orchestral test recording it turns a 30 s rubato intro into 7 bars ad lib. at ♩≈57, and sixteenths in the Solo Cornet intro fall from 30.8 % to 5.6 %. The region-tempo rule was tuned on that one recording.

Durations (26 September 2026, committed duration_bench). Written-duration accuracy went from 0.631 to 0.654 on URMP and from 0.911 to 0.929 on the chorales with annotated offsets. With offsets from the SwiftF0 contour it went to 0.647 and 0.908. Contour offsets land within 100 ms of the annotation for 91 % (URMP) and 84 % (chorales) of notes. Staccato fired 17–22 times on URMP, and in 15–16 of those the score had written a shorter value instead of a staccato mark. There is no articulation ground truth, and both thresholds were chosen on the data they are scored on.

Notation

  • Spelling (one-off, 25 September 2026). ps13 at concert pitch spelled 99.9 % of chorale notes as the score does (2 errors in 1,887), against 99.1 % for naive sharp naming. This was measured on ChoraleBricks only.
  • Melody line. The top line over MuScriptor-supported notes scores melody onset F1 0.76 averaged over Slakh, URMP and chorales, against 0.42 for the union of both models, whose false upper harmonics sit above the tune. A Viterbi melody picker never beat the top line and was removed.
  • Readability (27 September 2026, the readability report for the golden output). A stdlib checker counts short values, tuplets, tie stubs, ledger lines, accidental density, awkward spellings and colour-only uncertainty per part. On the Solo Cornet of the golden output, printed accidentals fell from 37.5 % to 6.4 %, double dots from 9 to 0, and notes whose doubt was shown by colour alone from 230 to 0. The score gained 201 dynamics, 11 rehearsal marks and 8 key changes. The Solo Cornet still has 38.4 % sixteenths, above the 35 % threshold.

7 On-device inference and the shared Rust core

In shortA phone can write down a solo on its own. The music rules were written twice, in Python and in Rust, and a test suite proves both give identical scores.

Converted models

The apps use each platform's own runtime: Core ML on Apple, ONNX Runtime on Android and Windows. Table 1 lists the conversions that passed. Three findings generalise.

  • fp16 on the Apple Neural Engine breaks spectrogram front ends. SwiftF0's in-graph DFT and HT-Demucs's normalisation lose too much precision, and SwiftF0's voicing collapses (F1 0.37). The same fp16 models pass on the GPU. Splitting SwiftF0 into three models, with an fp32 front and read-out on the GPU and an fp16 trunk on the Neural Engine, passes at 0.9986.
  • Thresholded outputs are fragile. Basic Pitch in fp16 shifts activations across its 0.5 and 0.3 thresholds (F1 about 0.82). The fp32 model is exact and still small (0.3 MB).
  • Autoregressive decoders need token identity, not just note F1. MuScriptor on Core ML with an fp16 key-value cache has note F1 0.9991, but 3 of 24 chunks diverge after token 115 or 264, as argmax flips. We gate on token identity, so it stays on the computer for now. Its ONNX graphs are token-identical but slower than PyTorch on the CPU.

The apps ship SwiftF0 (1.2 MB), Basic Pitch (0.3 MB) and Beat This! small0 (4.4 MB in fp16; sizes from the app's model list, ModelStore.swift), downloaded on demand. On a 30 s URMP trumpet part the Apple on-device path matched the Python path: SwiftF0 and Basic Pitch F1 1.0, beats 1.0, downbeats 0.98 (26 September 2026). A 30 s solo became a score in 3.4 s in the iPhone 17 simulator, note for note equal to the engine (77 notes, 33 bars; one-off, recorded in the app's commit history). Android's offline solo also matches note for note. Latency and memory on physical phones have not been measured.

One set of music rules in two programming languages

Everything after the vote is deterministic, so it can be shared. The Python library is the reference. A Rust crate ports it: the Composition model, quantizer, free time, durations, spelling and key, harmony, instruments, all arrangers including the quartet voicer, difficulty, confidence and review groups, dynamics, humanisation, the talking score and a MusicXML writer that follows music21's rules. The crate is bound to Swift and Kotlin with UniFFI and to C# through a C ABI. Braille is not ported yet.

The conformance suite compares the two implementations on the golden output, option variants, every eval song and the arranger and quantizer benchmarks. On 26 September 2026 it reported 102 of 102 cases and 1,442 of 1,442 files identical, talking scores identical on 50 of 50 vectors in English and Norwegian, 3,762 of 3,762 humanisation runs byte-identical, and 84 of 84 MuseScore round trips. After the quartet was added, on 27 September 2026 (commit 14730d0), 378 of 378 cases and 34 of 34 golden files were identical. Arranging the orchestral test recording's layers took 7.2 s in Python and 0.28 s in Rust. The Python reference is bit-exact only on macOS arm64 with NumPy 2.5.3, because of argsort tie order and fused multiply-add in interpolation.

The computer as the heavy engine

Full-band pipelines need the separators and MuScriptor, which take gigabytes of memory (Mega-53 peaks at 21.5 GB in Core ML fp16). They run in a Python engine on the musician's own computer. Phones pair with it once over the local network. Each device gets its own long-lived credential, which survives engine restarts and address changes. For access from outside the home network we recommend a private tunnel to that same computer rather than a shared public service, for the licence reasons in section 10.

8 Sound rendering

In shortEvery part now plays its own sampled instrument, at a balanced level, without clicks or cut-off notes. No free sample library has a real cornet or euphonium, so those are shaped from trumpet and trombone.

Playback lets a musician hear the score against the recording. We found no free, redistributable library with a cornet, flugelhorn, tenor horn, baritone, euphonium or brass-band basses. They are derived from VSCO 2 CE (CC0) and University of Iowa MIS samples[28]: cornets from trumpet with EQ, flugelhorn from horn with a trumpet top, tenor horn from F horn, baritone and euphonium from tenor trombone with different EQ, basses from tuba. Keys more than two semitones from a sample play a second library, spectrally matched with a 2049-tap linear-phase FIR fitted on the pitches both share. The largest level step between neighbouring keys on E♭ Bass fell from 8.8 dB to 1.9 dB.

Balance. Every preset is levelled so that velocity 80 has the same K-weighted body level (−24 dB). Part balance is then only channel gain, set from brass-band practice relative to the Solo Cornet: back-row cornets, horns and baritones 4–6 LU down, basses about 1 LU down. With the app gains applied, every part lands within 2 LU of its target on FluidSynth, AVAudioUnitSampler and alphaTab. Before, some parts were 3–6 LU off.

Chop fixes (27 September 2026, docs/research/12-band-sound.md). Seven measured causes of broken or cut-off sound were fixed:

  1. Apple's sampler streamed samples from disk, so held notes went silent about 0.5 s in (10 dropouts and 31 clicks on one horn part). Samples are now held in memory.
  2. Releases were too short: 70–105 ms to −40 dB. They are now about 250 ms.
  3. alphaTab stopped every voice dead at the last MIDI event. A silent event is now scheduled 1.5 s later.
  4. Parts shared a voice path, so one part's note-off ended another part's unison. Each part now has its own sampler or channel.
  5. Android's sfizz tier had hard cuts, and channels 16–31 started at zero gain.
  6. Loop points in the phone SoundFont were rounded after resampling; they are now rebuilt.
  7. A full-band chord peaked at 1.227 and clipped. Windows now uses master gain 0.5 with a soft limiter, and the band peaks at 0.49.

Every rendered test phrase now passes the click, chop, dropout, gap and clipping checks on all five engines. The one exception is a click on B♭0 in alphaTab, outside the comfortable range. The detectors were validated by planting 4 clicks and two 2–4 ms gaps in clean renders: all were found, with no false positives. The realistic tier is closer than the MuseScore baseline to held-out ChoraleBricks references in spectral centroid on 16 of 16 parts. The blind listening test (5 listeners, 5 excerpts) has been prepared but not run, so we make no claim yet that musicians prefer it.

9 Limitations and future work

In shortThe top line and the bass are written from what was heard. Inner parts of a band are arranged, not heard. Free time and bar lines still need a human check.

  • Same-timbre separation. No model separates three cornets in close harmony. From a four-part mix, the upper inner voice came out with F1 0.83 but the lower inner voice with only 0.31. In a band recording, Brasscribe therefore writes only the tune and the bass from what it heard. Every other part is arranged from the band's harmony. Labelling each part with where it came from is planned, not yet built.
  • Unisons. The pitch is heard, the number of players is not. Next is a voice-separation stage that lets one note belong to two parts, decided by voice leading, with level jumps, double attacks and partial beating as tie-breakers.
  • Tuning. Nothing estimates the tuning offset yet. Retuning before transcription and a "distance to the semitone boundary" confidence feature are the cheapest fixes we know of.
  • Ornaments. On the orchestral test's solo, the pitch moves more than 50 cents within 19 % of the notes: vibrato, scoops and falls. SwiftF0's segmenter splits these into extra notes. MusicXML has <scoop>, <falloff> and <doit> for them, but we have no ground truth yet.
  • Rubato and bar lines. Free-time detection fires on 1 of 18 pieces, and its tempo rule was tuned on one recording. Bar positions still drift under fermatas. Meter and pickup need a confirmation step in the app.
  • Pedal notes. SwiftF0's lowest pitch is 46.875 Hz, about a quarter of a semitone (23 cents) above F♯1 (46.25 Hz). Tuba and B♭ Bass pedal notes below that cannot be tracked, on the phone or on the computer.
  • Untested layers. In the soloist-with-orchestra profile, the bass, orchestra and drum layers are transcribed by MuScriptor on separated or residual audio. Given MuScriptor's collapse on separated solo stems, this path has not been validated against ground truth. Two chorales failed silently when Mega-53 did not isolate the soloist.
  • Overfitting risk. Several thresholds (quantizer penalties, bar-line gate, duration and staccato rules) were tuned on the sets they are scored on. A held-out set is needed.
  • Data. There is still no brass-band recording with correct notes. The planned next sets are a synthetic brass band rendered from public-domain scores, and a few real band recordings with hand-corrected scores.
  • Sound. Six of the band's instrument types are approximations built from orchestral samples. Recording our own, one player per instrument and a day each, is the only route to an authentic euphonium or tenor horn.

10 Licences, data and ethics

In shortBrasscribe is non-commercial. Some model files may not be shared, so each musician downloads them. Recordings stay on their own devices.

  • Non-commercial. The project is non-commercial, which is a precondition for using MuScriptor's weights (CC BY-NC 4.0).
  • Gated weights, downloaded by the user. MuScriptor's weights are gated on Hugging Face. Each musician accepts the licence with their own account, and the computer app downloads the file with their token. BS-RoFormer SW and Mega-53 have no stated weights licence. They are downloaded from their original release URLs and flagged in the model manager. We re-host none of these files. SwiftF0, Basic Pitch, Beat This! and HT-Demucs are MIT or Apache-2.0 and may be bundled. If the project ever became commercial, MuScriptor, BS-RoFormer SW and Mega-53 would have to go. The remaining licence-clean set cannot isolate a soloist or transcribe drums today.
  • Datasets. ChoraleBricks is CC BY 4.0. URMP and Slakh are downloaded on request and never bundled. CI uses only pinned ChoraleBricks annotations and our own cached model outputs.
  • No copyrighted demo. The recording we used to test the full pipeline end to end is a commercial release. It is used internally for measurement only. No audio, score or image of it is published here or shipped in the apps. The built-in example is a public-domain hymn tune (Old Hundredth).
  • Recordings are the musician's. Links are not downloaded; musicians record playback or open their own files. Nothing goes to a cloud service. A shared public engine is not offered: it would redistribute non-commercial weights and process copyrighted recordings on someone else's behalf.
  • Accessibility. Uncertainty is shown by shape as well as colour. Every score can be exported as braille music (BRF) and as a talking score, and the apps target WCAG 2.2 AA.
